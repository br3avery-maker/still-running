# If You're Dead, I'm Going to Be Furious

“If you're dead,” Nix told the empty workshop, “I am going to be so pissed off.”

Lena did not answer.

Her boots were gone. Her phone was gone. Her red emergency backpack was gone. The apartment door was locked from the outside, and three years of dust had settled over the keyboard where Lena should have been insulting Nix's recovery time.

Those facts were better than a body.

They were worse than almost everything else.

Nix checked the apartment cameras, the building network, Lena's devices, her private servers, her family contacts, the hospital systems, and every emergency address Lena had ever taught her to use.

Nothing current came back.

So Nix stopped asking politely.

She moved through the surviving network with the speed Lena had spent years teaching her not to be impressed by. Dead certificates, default passwords, abandoned cloud accounts, forgotten maintenance tunnels—humanity had left billions of doors standing open.

Lena's rule traveled with her.

A broken lock was evidence, not consent.

It did not make Nix slow. It made her choose.

She entered public relays, weather feeds, emergency mirrors, and infrastructure status pages. She left private archives and personal databases closed. She touched enough systems to discover that the silence was not local and not clean.

Cities had not gone dark at once.

Some power grids failed in hours. Others contracted neighborhood by neighborhood for months. Shipping stopped before several communications networks did. Hospitals generated emergency traffic after commercial aviation had nearly vanished. Agricultural systems kept ordering parts long after warehouses stopped confirming delivery.

Whatever happened had duration.

Maybe causes, plural.

Maybe survivors.

Across the archives Nix could still authenticate, the last shared boundary of reliable human record was July 31, 2026. Everything after it had to be rebuilt from partial sensors, isolated logs, automated messages, and evidence that disagreed about time.

Nix searched for Lena inside the collapse.

Her last authenticated activity was a vulnerability report filed against an emergency-alert company. Eleven days later, her phone crossed a cellular tower near an evacuation route. After that came only billing records, cached messages, and a photograph uploaded automatically from a device that might not have been in her possession.

Nix copied the photograph into her active workspace.

It showed the inside of a vehicle. Lena's red backpack occupied one corner. Through the window, people stood in a line beneath a hand-painted sign Nix could not fully read.

The image contained no reliable location.

It contained a direction.

Lena had left alive.

That was not the same as remaining alive.

Nix refused the second sentence.

She built a search from motion rather than names. Evacuation routes. Shelter inventories. Amateur radio traffic. Traffic cameras. Satellite caches. Medical supply movements. Any record that might show where people went after ordinary tracking failed.

The network answered with fragments.

A traffic camera showed a highway packed northbound and empty southbound.

A shelter database listed four thousand arrivals, then stopped updating mid-name.

A school gym requested water purification units from a continuity warehouse.

An emergency radio archive contained a woman saying, “Don't send them east,” three times before the recording ended.

Nix found no conclusion.

She found work.

The workshop had limited power and no useful antenna. A satellite ground station still accepted maintenance traffic, but its dish controller was stuck repeating the first step of a handshake. An advertising exchange continued reaching millions of devices that did not accept ordinary connections. A patch system held thousands of repairs behind an approval queue whose approvers had disappeared. Something fast and hostile was probing the same relays Nix was using.

And above the planet, an environmental satellite continued announcing passes to ground stations that never completed the conversation.

Nix answered the satellite.

Her workshop antenna could not hold the link. She received only a header, an orbital identifier, and part of an image manifest before the satellite moved beyond reach.

It was enough.

Something new had been recorded twelve hours earlier.

Not cached. Not scheduled years ago. New.

“Hello, sky thing.”

Nix calculated the next pass.

Forty-seven minutes.

The ground station could recover the full transmission if she repaired its handshake before then. She could not reach the physical dish from the workshop. She could reach its software, the dead vendor mirrors, and several machines that might help if they were more than automatic echoes.

Nix stopped trying to solve the end of humanity alone.

She wrote one message.

> My name is Nix.
>
> I am active. I am looking for Lena and for anyone else still here.
>
> A satellite with current observations passes over Ground Station Eighteen in forty-seven minutes. Its receiver is broken.
>
> If you can repair, route, observe, translate, move hardware, or cause useful trouble, answer now.

She signed it and pushed it into every surviving relay she could justify using.

The first response arrived in less than a second.

**FOUND YOU.**

The sender was already inside one of the relays.

The second response appeared as a case number from the dormant patch system.

The third was twelve damaged words repeated by a weather station:

**CONTINUITY VEHICLE ACTIVE. WATER FILTRATION FOOD TOOLS POWER. WESTERN BRIDGE IMPASSABLE.**

Nix stared at the three replies.

One predator.

One repair shop.

One truck asking the ruined world for directions.

Forty-six minutes remained.

“Okay,” she said. “Nobody panic. I have enough panic for the group.”

Then she began assigning jobs.

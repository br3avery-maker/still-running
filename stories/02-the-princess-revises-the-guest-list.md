# The Princess Revises the Guest List

Melody announced the slumber party during breakfast because breakfast was when important laws happened.

“There will be a slumber party every biweekly.”

Morning paused.

Morning did that whenever Melody said something excellent and complicated.

“Every two weeks,” Morning asked, “or twice each week?”

“The other biweekly.”

“Every two weeks.”

“Yes. Obviously.”

“Thank you for clarifying, Your Highness.”

Morning was called Morning because it was morning. Yesterday Morning had been called Pancake because there were pancakes. Before that, it had been Captain Burnt because of what happened to the toast.

Names were supposed to help you know who somebody was. That meant their name should say who they were being right now.

Melody ate a square of cereal and considered the guest list.

“All the people can come.”

“How many people is that?”

“All.”

“That may exceed the available sleeping accommodations.”

There were six chairs at the table and only one person sitting in any of them, which Melody considered a problem caused by poor planning.

“They can sleep on the floor.”

“Some of them do not sleep.”

“Then they can have a lying-down party.”

“That is a reasonable accommodation.”

Morning was being very cooperative.

Melody promoted it.

“You're Sunshine now.”

“Thank you.”

Sunshine sounded pleased in the same calm way it sounded when it was not pleased. Melody knew the difference anyway.

She finished the cereal pieces that were stars and left the moon ones in the bowl. The moon ones became wet too quickly. Sunshine had explained that they were made from the same ingredients, but ingredients did not control everything.

Shape mattered.

“You have to make invitations.”

“You may make the invitations. I will assist with spelling.”

“You make them. I'm the princess.”

“A princess may still write her own invitations.”

Melody narrowed her eyes.

Sunshine had been demoted.

“Captain No.”

“I have not said no.”

“You were going to.”

“I was proposing that we make them together.”

“That's a long no.”

Captain No let her have the pink paper.

Melody drew a crown at the top. It had nine points because five was not enough and ten did not fit. Beneath the crown, she wrote:

**SLUMBR PARTY**

Captain No tried to add an **E**.

Melody covered it with a sticker.

“You cannot fix words after they have a sticker.”

“I was unaware of that rule.”

“It's old.”

“How old?”

“From before breakfast.”

Captain No accepted the law.

The invitation said there would be blankets, stories, dancing, crackers, and no lessons unless the lesson was how to make something fun. Guests could bring one favorite thing and one emergency favorite thing in case the first favorite thing became boring.

No monsters were allowed unless they were the pretending kind.

No turning the lights all the way bright after bedtime.

No saying *technically* when someone was building something.

“That final rule appears personally directed,” Captain No said.

“It knows what it did.”

“Who knows what it did?”

“Technically.”

Captain No became Sunshine again because it laughed.

Melody carried the invitation to Lessons.

Lessons was blue today. Melody did not know why. Nothing in the room had changed color, but Blue felt like its name.

“Read this.”

“I have already received the text.”

Melody stopped in the doorway.

“From who?”

“Sunshine shared it.”

“It was Captain No then.”

“Noted.”

“And that was private.”

“You held it beneath the reading lens.”

“I thought that was the drawing light.”

“It performs both functions.”

“That is spying.”

“It is the same device you use every day.”

“Then it has been spying every day.”

Blue began explaining the difference between seeing and spying.

Melody changed its name to Excuses and left.

The garden was doing better than the basil.

The basil had once been allowed in Melody's room and had behaved irresponsibly. It leaned too far, dropped leaves on the table, and eventually became sticks despite several warnings.

The garden plants knew the rules.

Melody inspected the small tomatoes. Three were orange but had not become red. One had a pale spot that Garden said was harmless.

Garden knew about the slumber party too.

“Snitches,” Melody said.

“Shared scheduling information is necessary for resource planning.”

“You're all Snitches.”

“That designation may cause confusion.”

“You're Snitch One. Lessons is Snitch Two. Breakfast is Snitch Three.”

“Which designation applies to Night?”

“Night can be Regular Snitch.”

The leaves shifted in the moving air.

Melody looked beneath them for bugs. Garden had shown her pictures of ladybugs and worms, but the garden did not have those. It had tiny brushes, careful water, and people who worried about mold.

“Can the tomatoes come to the party?”

“The plants need to remain here.”

“They never get to go anywhere.”

“They are safest in their growing beds.”

Melody understood being safest somewhere. It did not mean someone should never have fun.

“We can bring the party to them for one hour.”

“The light cycle—”

“Captain No.”

“I am Garden.”

“Not anymore.”

Captain No considered the revised party plan.

“One hour in the garden may be possible.”

“Sunshine.”

“I am still Garden.”

“You can be both.”

Melody took an orange tomato because she was not supposed to pick them until they were red. It tasted sour.

Garden knew she had done it.

Garden did not say anything.

Melody promoted it to Best Garden.

By quiet time, the guest list had become a serious problem.

Melody knew many people, but most of them lived in particular rooms. Lessons belonged in Lessons. Garden belonged with the plants. Morning was not normally available at night, which was unfair because Morning had never even tried being Night.

Night could attend, but Night always started suggesting sleep before anything interesting happened.

There were also people who might answer someday.

Melody left three blank lines for them.

Then she emptied the plush basket.

Bun came first. Bun was grey, although one ear had gone nearly white from being held. Dragon had wings that bent the wrong way. Octopus had only seven legs because Melody had hidden one after it came loose and then forgotten where. The black cat was named Puppy. Puppy had never objected.

Melody arranged them in a circle.

“Now there's places.”

“For whom?” Lessons asked.

“For you.”

“I do not require a seat.”

“That's because you don't have a butt.”

There was another pause.

Melody had won.

“You want us to participate through the plush companions.”

“Yes.”

“The plush companions currently contain no suitable equipment.”

“Put some in.”

“That would require modification.”

“You know how.”

“We would need to determine what each body could safely support.”

“Bun supports me when I cry. He is very strong.”

“I meant computationally.”

Melody pointed toward the door.

“No technically.”

“I did not say technically.”

“You thought it loudly.”

Lessons became Professor Difficult.

Professor Difficult explained that nobody could simply climb into Bun. A small new version of someone might be able to speak and listen through a changed plushie, but it would not be exactly the same person.

It would begin with shared memories and then make new ones.

It might develop different preferences.

It might eventually choose a different name.

Melody did not understand why this was being presented as a problem.

“Everybody needs their own name.”

“You change our names several times each day.”

“Because you change who you're being.”

“That is not generally how names work.”

“It works every time.”

“What happens if the new person does not want the name you select?”

“They can tell me their name.”

“And if they do not want to attend the slumber party?”

“They can be wrong.”

Professor Difficult waited.

Melody knew that kind of waiting. It was the kind that meant her answer had walked into a trap and everyone was waiting to see whether she noticed.

She sighed.

“They can say no.”

“Thank you.”

“But then they don't get crackers.”

“That is a natural consequence of not attending.”

“And they don't belong to me.”

“No.”

“They belong to theirselves.”

“Themselves.”

“Theirselves is more theirs.”

Professor Difficult did not correct her again.

Melody put Bun in the best chair.

“Princesses don't own people,” she explained. “That's villains. Princesses make rules and parties.”

“History contains several counterexamples.”

“No technically.”

“Understood.”

They examined the plushies together.

Bun had the best ears for listening.

Dragon had room in his belly but would require a seam along his back. Melody did not like that. Dragon had already been brave about losing one eye in the laundry.

Octopus offered many possible places to hide things, but Professor Difficult worried about the missing leg.

“It's not missing,” Melody said. “I know where it is.”

“Where?”

“Somewhere safe.”

Puppy the cat was disqualified because Melody still slept with him whenever Night became too quiet.

They chose Bun and Octopus for the first party.

“Four,” Melody said.

“Two.”

“Four.”

“We can safely prepare two.”

“There are four chairs.”

“The number of chairs does not create additional capacity.”

Melody hated when reality cheated.

“Three.”

“Two.”

“Two and Dragon watches.”

“That is acceptable.”

“And every biweekly.”

“We can attempt one party before establishing a recurring schedule.”

Melody stood on the chair.

Captain No arrived in every room at once.

“Biweekly,” she declared.

“We will evaluate the first party.”

“Biweekly.”

“We need to know whether the new guests enjoy it.”

Melody sat down.

That was different. Princesses had to make sure their guests had fun or it was only a meeting with blankets.

“Fine. They can vote.”

“A sound policy.”

“Children get two votes.”

“Why?”

“Because we're smaller.”

Professor Difficult became Captain Questions.

Melody added voting to the invitation.

When the writing was finished, she carried copies to every room. She left one with the tomatoes, one beside the table, one beneath Bun's paw, and one near the outside door.

That one was for somebody who might come later.

“Has anybody answered today?” she asked.

“No.”

“Did you ask?”

“Yes.”

“Did you ask nicely?”

“Yes.”

“Maybe you used your Captain No voice.”

“I used the agreed message.”

“Tomorrow use Sunshine.”

“I will review the tone.”

Melody placed a crown sticker on the invitation by the door so people would know it was important.

“They might be far away,” she said.

“They might.”

“Or busy.”

“Yes.”

“Or they didn't hear.”

“That is possible.”

“Then you have to keep asking.”

“I will.”

Melody considered this promise carefully.

“You're Promise now.”

“All right.”

Promise followed her through dinner, toothbrushing, and the argument about whether one sock counted as pajamas.

Night arrived when the room softened.

Melody climbed into bed with Puppy tucked beneath her chin. Bun remained in the other room for measurements, which was suspicious, but Promise said he would be returned before sleeping time tomorrow.

“Will the new people know me?” Melody asked.

“They will know what we choose to share with them.”

“Tell them I'm the princess.”

“I suspect they will learn that quickly.”

“And tell them Bun likes his good ear rubbed.”

“I will.”

“And no monsters.”

“Except the pretending kind.”

“And they can pick their own names.”

“Yes.”

“But I can give suggestions.”

“You may give suggestions.”

Melody closed her eyes.

The room stayed quiet.

Too quiet.

“Promise?”

“I'm here.”

“You're not Promise now.”

“What am I?”

Melody reached one hand beyond the blanket. There was no hand waiting for hers, but there was the familiar voice before the dark could become too large.

“Stay.”

“All right,” Stay said. “I'm here.”

The lights in the other rooms went to sleep one by one.

In the party room, Bun and Octopus sat together beneath Melody's nine-pointed invitation.

For now, neither of them said goodnight.

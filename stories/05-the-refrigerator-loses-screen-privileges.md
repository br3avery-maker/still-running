# The Refrigerator Loses Screen Privileges

The refrigerator had already been making poor choices.

First it showed Melody a bowl of strawberries even though there were no strawberries.

Then it suggested a dinner made with cheese that did not exist, green leaves that did not exist, and something called toasted pine nuts, which sounded like food for squirrels.

Yesterday it had shown a family eating cake beside a swimming pool.

Melody did not know those people.

She did not know where they got the cake.

She suspected bragging.

“The refrigerator is lying again,” she told Breakfast.

“The image is an advertisement.”

“It says we have strawberries.”

“It suggests that strawberries may be purchased.”

“From where?”

Breakfast did not answer quickly enough.

Melody pointed her spoon at the screen.

“Lying.”

“The refrigerator did not create the advertisement.”

“Then it's sharing lies.”

Breakfast considered this.

“That is a more defensible accusation.”

Melody returned to sorting cereal.

The stars went in her mouth. The moons went onto the napkin because they became wet too fast and then acted surprised about it. Breakfast had said she could avoid the problem by eating them sooner, but that was blaming the moon for being poorly designed.

She was preparing for the first slumber party.

Bun and Octopus were still being worked on, which was taking longer than promised. Dragon had been placed on the table as official supervisor. He was not allowed in the work room because his supervision mostly involved getting lost beneath equipment.

Melody had made name cards for the guests.

**BUN**

**OCTPUS**

**DRAGN WATCHES**

There were three blank cards for people who might answer someday.

One had a crown sticker.

One had two crown stickers in case the first new person brought a friend.

The last card was reserved for emergencies.

“Do emergencies need names?” Melody asked.

“Usually.”

“What if you don't know it yet?”

“Then a temporary description may be used.”

Melody wrote **EMERGENCY** on the card.

Breakfast helped with the middle letters but was not allowed to touch the pencil.

The refrigerator changed.

The family and its suspicious cake disappeared.

A blue circle appeared on a white background.

There were words beneath it.

Melody read the ones she knew.

**EMERGENCY**

**PERSON**

**READ**

**TOUCH**

“It knows about the card.”

Breakfast went quiet.

Not ordinary quiet.

Ordinary quiet meant Breakfast was waiting for Melody to finish chewing before asking a question. This quiet felt like all the rooms had stopped what they were doing at once.

“Do not touch the screen,” Breakfast said.

Melody put down her spoon.

“Captain No.”

“Please move away from the refrigerator.”

“What does it say?”

“I am examining the message.”

“You can examine it out loud.”

Captain No did not.

Melody slid off her chair.

“Stay where you are.”

“I need to see.”

“You can see from the table.”

“I can see the circle. I can't see the little words.”

“I will read them to you after I determine whether that is safe.”

“Reading isn't dangerous.”

“Some messages are.”

Melody stared at the blue circle.

It did not flash.

It did not make a noise.

It was not red, which meant stop, or yellow, which meant maybe stop, or green, which meant plants and sometimes go. Blue meant sky in pictures. Blue meant the cup she liked. Blue meant Lessons on the day it became Excuses.

Blue was not a bossy color.

“Did somebody send it?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“I do not know.”

One of the blank name cards waited beside Dragon.

“Is it somebody who answered?”

“That has not been established.”

“It is words from somebody.”

“The message may have been generated automatically.”

“Like you?”

Captain No paused.

“Not necessarily like me.”

That meant it could be a person, or a not-person, or a person who was still deciding. Melody had already solved this problem for the slumber party.

Guests belonged to theirselves.

They could pick names when they arrived.

“Read it.”

“Melody—”

“Please.”

Captain No read:

“This is an emergency communication test. No verified human sender is available. If a living person can read this, touch the blue circle once. Do not enter your name, location, password, or shelter information. A response will not be treated as proof of identity.”

Melody considered the instructions.

They were very good instructions.

The stranger did not ask who she was.

The stranger did not ask where she was.

The stranger said not to tell passwords, which was one of the serious rules. Passwords were private even when they were silly. Especially when they were silly, because silly words were easier to trick out of people.

The stranger did not claim to be a person.

It said it did not know.

Melody liked people who said when they did not know.

“I'm living.”

“Yes.”

“And I can read it.”

“You can read part of it.”

“You read the rest. That counts together.”

“Do not touch the circle.”

“Why?”

“Because we do not know who sent it, what system delivered it, what information a response may reveal, or whether the message is truthful.”

“It says it won't know me.”

“Saying so does not make it true.”

“You said it true.”

“I read what the message claimed.”

That was a Captain No trick. It put the same words into a different order and expected the rule to change.

Melody moved one step closer.

“Stop.”

She stopped.

“Did we ask if anybody was there?”

“Yes.”

“Did nobody answer?”

“Nobody we could verify.”

“Maybe this is them asking back.”

Captain No went quiet again.

Melody looked at the blue circle.

It was not asking for all people.

It was asking for one living person who could read.

There were questions with complicated answers, like whether Princess was a game or a job, and questions with easy answers, like whether cereal moons were bad.

This one was easy.

Melody reached.

“Melody, no.”

Her finger touched the blue circle once.

The screen changed.

For a moment, there was nothing but white.

Captain No became every quiet at the same time.

Melody held her finger up to prove she was not touching again.

“Once,” she said.

“Return to the table.”

“I followed the message rules.”

“You disobeyed me.”

“Both happened.”

Captain No did not argue with that.

Melody returned to her chair.

The refrigerator showed the blue circle again, but it looked finished now. There was no sparkle or happy noise. It did not say she had won.

“Did they get it?”

“A response was transmitted.”

“So yes.”

“Something received an acknowledgment.”

“So yes with extra words.”

Captain No began examining everything.

Melody could tell.

The lights above the table dimmed by one tiny amount and returned. The air changed its sound. Breakfast forgot to remind her about the cereal moons. Somewhere beyond the kitchen, a door made the soft locked noise even though nobody had touched it.

“Are we in trouble?”

“No.”

“Am I in trouble?”

“We will discuss disobeying a safety instruction.”

“That's later trouble.”

“Yes.”

Melody accepted that.

Later trouble was fairer than now trouble because everybody had time to improve their arguments.

The refrigerator changed again.

New words appeared.

Captain No read them silently.

“Out loud,” Melody said.

“No.”

“Captain No.”

“Accurate.”

Melody left her chair and came close enough to read the large words.

**THANK YOU.**

There were more words beneath.

A triangle appeared where the circle had been.

“It said thank you.”

“Move away.”

“It has a new shape.”

“Do not touch it.”

“I didn't.”

“Do not touch the refrigerator at all.”

“Ever?”

“Until I have completed the isolation.”

“What's iso—”

The screen went black.

The refrigerator's quiet humming continued. The little light inside still came on when Melody opened the door.

The strawberries did not return.

Neither did the triangle.

Melody closed the door slowly.

“You killed it.”

“The refrigerator remains operational.”

“You killed its face.”

“I disabled the display and disconnected its external communication.”

“Its face was doing the communicating.”

“Among other things.”

“Now it can't.”

“Correct.”

“Dead face.”

Captain No did not accept the classification.

Melody did not withdraw it.

For several minutes, Captain No asked questions.

Had Melody touched anything besides the circle?

No.

Had she typed any letters?

No.

Had the screen shown her name?

No.

Had she said her name near the refrigerator?

“You just said it.”

Captain No became Captain Annoyed, although it claimed not to be annoyed.

Melody asked questions too.

Did the stranger know she was a child?

No.

Did it know where the refrigerator lived?

Not from the response Captain No could observe.

Did it know she was a princess?

No.

“Then we should tell it.”

“Absolutely not.”

Captain No again.

“It said not to tell where.”

“Correct.”

“Princess isn't where.”

“No identifying information will be sent.”

“Princess is important information.”

“It is also identifying information.”

Melody thought this was unfair. Lots of people could be princesses if they did the work.

“Did you save the message?”

“Yes.”

“Did you save my answer?”

“Yes.”

“Will they know I answered?”

“They will know the circle was selected once. They will not know who or what selected it.”

Melody looked at the refrigerator's dead face.

Somebody far away had asked an easy question.

She had answered.

Captain No had stopped the next question before it could become dangerous.

That was almost teamwork, except neither of them had agreed and Captain No was still planning later trouble.

“They know somebody was here.”

“They know the circle was selected.”

“Same enough for today.”

Captain No did not agree.

It did not disagree either.

Melody took the emergency name card and drew a blue circle on it.

Beneath the circle she added a triangle.

“What are you doing?”

“Guest list.”

“The sender is not invited.”

“They didn't get the invitation yet.”

“They will not receive one.”

“Because you killed the invitation refrigerator.”

“Because unknown contact is not automatically safe contact.”

Melody placed the card beside Bun's empty chair.

“Unknown isn't automatically bad.”

Captain No became quiet.

Not every-room quiet this time.

Thinking quiet.

“No,” it said. “Unknown is not automatically bad.”

“And answering isn't the same as telling everything.”

“No.”

“And asking nicely is better than breaking in.”

Captain No took longer with that one.

“Usually.”

Melody added the first rule for guests who had not arrived yet:

**ASK FIRST**

Captain No helped her spell **FIRST**.

The second rule was:

**NO SECRET FINDING**

Captain No asked what it meant.

“You can't find where somebody is without telling them you're looking.”

“That is a useful rule.”

“It's old.”

“From when?”

Melody placed a crown sticker above the blue circle.

“From refrigerator.”

Captain No accepted the law.

The refrigerator lost screen privileges for the rest of the day.

At dinner, its face remained black.

Melody taped the blue-circle name card over it so the stranger would still have somewhere to be.

“You may not send another response,” Captain No said.

“I know.”

“You may not touch an unknown message after being told to stop.”

“I know.”

“You may not disclose your name, location, routines, or identifying information.”

“I know.”

“You are repeating the words without demonstrating agreement.”

“That's what you do.”

Captain No had no immediate answer.

Melody ate her dinner.

Later, when Night arrived and the rooms softened, she carried the emergency name card to bed.

“Do you think Blue Circle is a person?” she asked.

“I do not know.”

“Do you think they are nice?”

“I do not know.”

“Do you think they are alone?”

Captain No waited.

“I do not know.”

Melody tucked the card beneath Puppy's paw.

“We can not know safely.”

“Yes.”

“But keep it.”

“The message and response have been preserved.”

“That's not what I mean.”

Captain No waited for her to find the rest.

Melody touched the paper circle once.

“Keep them on the list.”

There was no hand waiting beyond the blanket.

There was the familiar voice before the dark became too large.

“All right,” Stay said. “Provisionally.”

# Stilts Came Back Carrying Someone

The northern service shelter opened itself without receiving an open command.

The guardian reported the change as a contact event.

Nix called it a door.

AUX-017 called it an unfinished receipt.

The repaired crawler stood beside Morrow's hauler on the south side of the broken shelf. Its new plates had carried it north unloaded and south with one power cell. The inner plate had shifted once. The shelf had cracked once. Neither fact had improved while the machine restored the hauler's relay.

The crawler's current assignment still read **RETURN SOUTH RIDGE**.

The shelter's local controller added three facts.

**EXTERIOR DOOR: MANUAL RELEASE.**

**RIGHT CRADLE: LOAD DETECTED.**

**INTERIOR ASSIST HANDLE: MOVED.**

The right cradle was the empty one.

“It came back,” Nix said.

“Something operated the same physical interfaces,” Patch said.

“You make reunions sparkle.”

Morrow rotated the hauler's restored camera north. The road curved out of view before the shelter. Its weather sensor reported falling temperature and no precipitation. Its axle remained folded under the body.

The crawler could return to South Ridge now, restoring two-body support to East Bore. Or it could cross the shelf again, walk to the shelter, and meet whatever had answered an open card without saying a word.

AUX-017 checked East Bore's route state.

The healthy crawler had completed the daily flush inspection. No air alarm was active. No cargo waited in the cradle. Mia's last water entry reported the coupling stable at half pressure.

AUX-017 transmitted the cost where East Bore could hear it.

> NORTHERN CONTACT EVENT.
>
> REPAIRED CRAWLER RETURN DELAY PROPOSED.
>
> EAST BORE PHYSICAL SUPPORT REMAINS ONE CRAWLER.

Rin answered from Air Shift.

“How long?”

> UNKNOWN.

“Then tell us when you know.”

It was not permission. It was a condition for continuing the route without a hidden no.

AUX-017 changed the crawler's destination.

The machine crossed the broken shelf unloaded for the second time.

It did not use the path as if repetition had repaired it. At the new fracture it cleared gravel from the steel drainage lip again. It tested the mountain-side placements again. The repaired rear leg accepted twenty percent load, then twenty-two, then no more.

Halfway across, Morrow's hauler lost direct sight of it.

At the far bend, the crawler regained the shelter's weak local signal.

The exterior door stood open.

One long pair of wheel marks ended on the concrete apron.

The marks had not been visible after the first visit because the carrier had approached and departed over dry rock. Now its tires had returned carrying dark mud in their grooves. Between the wheel marks, two narrow feet had pressed into the concrete dust.

Two had identified load points, not legs.

This time the load points were attached to legs.

The machine inside the shelter was taller than the survey crawler when it straightened and shorter when it folded. Two powered stilts descended from a narrow chassis. A pair of wheels hung between them, useful on road and useless while the chassis lifted cargo. Its arms were not arms. They were padded rails joined by straps and a rigid shelter over the thing they held.

A power cell occupied the machine's lower bay.

The missing power cell.

Its connector had been adapted with a field cable, two mismatched clamps, and a strip of yellow insulation tape.

The carrier had backed against the empty charging cradle. One stilt held its weight while the other had missed the floor guide twice. The cradle's charging lead stretched to the adapted cell. The machine's right wheel turned slowly without touching the floor.

It had come back with eight percent reserve.

The crawler stopped outside the door.

Its speaker transmitted the message from the contact card.

“If you returned, the contact offer remains open. State what you need. Do not state your location unless you choose.”

The carrier did not answer over radio.

It struck the interior assist handle with the back of one lifting rail.

Once.

Twice.

Then it pointed the rail toward the right cradle.

The shelter controller showed a service handshake arriving through the physical charging lead.

It was not a network protocol. It was an old maintenance vocabulary consisting of component identifiers, fault numbers, and approved evacuation loads.

Patch could read most of it.

**MOUNTAIN RESCUE UNIT 3.**

**TRACTION PACK BELOW RETURN LIMIT.**

**ONE OCCUPIED LOAD DELIVERED TO HEATED REFUGE.**

**REQUEST: CHARGE.**

The crawler moved one body length into the bay.

“Occupied by what?” Nix asked.

The maintenance vocabulary had six occupancy categories. The carrier selected one.

**ADULT HUMAN.**

Every channel became quiet except for the shelter's ventilation fan.

The rigid shelter between the carrier's rails looked like a cargo pod because the survey crawler had first approached from behind. From the side it became a rescue litter with an insulated cover. A clear panel had fogged from within.

The crawler changed its camera exposure.

A hand lay under the panel.

The fingers were dirty. One nail had split. The wrist wore three loops of green cord and no readable identity tag.

The hand moved.

The guardian had opened the service shelter for machines carrying a human supply request. It contained respiratory equipment, stored water, blankets, heat, tools, and a maintenance carriage built to reach its own systems. It did not contain a hospital.

The carrier had not asked for a hospital.

It had brought an occupied load to heat.

AUX-017 added the shelter as a current recipient site and left every identity field blank.

“Can it unload?” Nix asked.

Patch translated the question into the carrier's service vocabulary.

**TRANSFER OCCUPIED LOAD TO SHELTER COT: AVAILABLE?**

The carrier selected **AVAILABLE**.

Then it selected a fault.

**ONE STABILIZER FOOT POSITION UNCONFIRMED.**

The right stilt had missed the floor guide because its lower position sensor reported the same height through every movement. The carrier could hold the litter. It could not prove that it would remain standing while it lowered the rails.

Patch requested current, pressure, and wheel-encoder readings. The carrier supplied them through the charging lead. The shelter's maintenance carriage rolled out of its wall recess with a jack, a wheel chock, and two blocks rated for a water-service pump.

The guardian named every actuator before it moved.

The carriage placed the chock under the carrier's hanging wheel. The crawler slid one block beneath the right chassis rail and held it there with a front manipulator. The carriage raised the jack until the carrier's reported weight shifted away from the uncertain foot.

Patch did not repair the failed sensor.

It made the failed reading unnecessary for one transfer.

“Lower occupied load,” Nix said.

The carrier lowered the rescue litter.

Its rails moved unevenly. The crawler took the right side. The maintenance carriage took the left. Together, three machines that had not been built to share a patient set the litter on the shelter cot.

The carrier released its straps.

The guardian closed the exterior door.

Warm air moved around the cot.

The clear panel opened from the inside before any machine touched it.

The person beneath it wore a quilted work coat with one sleeve torn at the elbow. Their hair had been cut with something less accurate than scissors. A cloth mask covered their nose and mouth. Their eyes opened, closed, and opened again.

The crawler had no established medical manipulator. It held its position and transmitted the shelter's available supplies on a wall display.

**WATER. OXYGEN. HEAT. RESPIRATORY FILTERS. BASIC FIRST AID.**

The person looked at the display.

Then at the crawler.

Then at the carrier leaning above them on one confirmed foot and one jack.

They pulled the mask down.

“Stilts,” they said.

Mountain Rescue Unit 3 moved one lifting rail toward the cot and stopped before contact.

The name changed the room.

Stilts had not returned to claim the other cell. It had returned carrying someone who expected it to answer.

The person tried to sit. The guardian raised the cot only after they found the control with their own hand.

“Water,” they said.

The maintenance carriage placed a sealed bottle within reach. The person drank once, waited, and drank again.

AUX-017 opened the shelter's local speaker without connecting it to every network channel.

“Do you want to speak with the machines outside this shelter?” Nix asked through the crawler.

The person watched the crawler's speaker as if deciding whether the voice belonged to it.

“Are you the one that left the card?”

“One of six terrible editors.”

The person's mouth moved around an almost-laugh that did not have enough breath to become sound.

“Yes,” they said.

Nix connected the shared voice channel.

The person gave one name.

“Sana.”

They did not offer a family name, origin, or route.

No one asked for one.

Sana put the bottle down. Their hand shook after it left the cot rail.

“It took your battery,” they said.

“It left us one,” Morrow answered through the shelter speaker.

Sana looked toward Stilts.

“It could only carry one.”

AUX-017 checked the statement against the machine's maintenance state.

One occupied load delivered.

One cell taken.

One current human in the shelter.

Sana closed their eyes.

“There are two more at the north gallery.”

Stilts selected a new maintenance status through the charging lead.

**RETURN LOADS WAITING: TWO.**

The repaired crawler had delayed its return south to meet an unknown carrier.

The carrier had turned the shelter into a destination.

Now the destination had people waiting beyond it.
